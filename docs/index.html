Consumer spending in Australia

Where does the money go?

Australian households now spend about $82 billion a month on the goods and services the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) tracks, about 35% more than in 2012 once price rises are removed. Twelve charts follow where it goes, how that has shifted over 30 years, how it differs from state to state, and what has stayed surprisingly steady.

Terms

Hover over any chart for exact figures. Where a chart can also be clicked, its subtitle says so.

Seasonally adjusted
Smoothed to remove the usual yearly pattern, such as the December rush, so one month can be compared fairly with another.
Current prices
Dollars of the day. Figures include price rises, so part of any increase is things costing more. Charts other than Chart 1 use current prices.
Price-adjusted
Past spending restated in August 2026 dollars, so a rise means people are buying more, not just paying more. Chart 1 uses this.
Tracked spending
What the ABS monthly indicator measures. It leaves out rent, education, communications, electricity and gas.
Wants and needs
“Wants” is our plain word for what the ABS calls discretionary spending, the kind people can more easily cut back on. “Needs” is the rest.
01

The big picture

How much do Australians spend, and on what?

Chart 1 · Total monthly spending

Spending has climbed from $61.1 billion a month in July 2012 to $82.3 billion in August 2026, up 35% after price rises are removed (all in August 2026 dollars). In the dollars of the day, the rise is 88%, from $43.7 billion, but much of that is prices.

The one sharp break came in 2020: spending fell from $67.6 billion in February to $52.6 billion in April, and was back above its February level by July.

Chart 2 · Where a dollar goes

Housing is by far the biggest slice: 24.6% of the $1,523 billion spent in 2025–26, well ahead of insurance, finance and other (13.8%), transport (10.3%) and recreation (10.2%).

Housing includes an estimated rent for people who own their home: the national accounts treat owners as if they rent it from themselves. Rent, real and estimated, is about 21% of all spending.

Housing leads. But has the order always looked like this? Part 2 follows the mix over time.

02

Composition over time

How has the mix of spending changed?

Chart 3 · Rank of each category, 40 years

Housing has ranked first in every year shown, from 1985–86 to 2025–26. Beneath it the order has reshuffled.

Education climbed from 12th to 9th, health from 9th to 7th, and insurance, finance and other from 4th to 2nd. Food slipped from 3rd to 5th and clothing from 8th to 10th.

Chart 4 · Monthly spending by category

Since 2013, eating out and health spending have each roughly doubled (+103% and +102% by 2025), with food up 72% and recreation up 71%.

Alcohol and tobacco is the odd one out: up only 11%, after peaking at $47.8 billion in 2022 and falling to $35.5 billion in 2025.

Chart 5 · The 2020 shock, cell by cell

In April 2020, eating out was 56% below a year earlier and transport 54% below. A year later they jumped by 99% and 83%, but only because they were compared with the weak months of 2020: in April 2021 eating out was still 13% below April 2019.

Since 2023 most cells are pale, so spending has mostly moved a few percentage points from the year before, usually upward.

Alcohol and tobacco is the exception: below a year earlier in 41 of the 44 months from January 2023 to August 2026. In August 2026 all nine categories were above a year earlier.

The mix moves, and a shock can move it fast. Part 3 asks whether every state moves together.

03

Across Australia

Does spending look the same in every state?

Chart 6 · How fast spending is growing

The Northern Territory (+8.2%) and Western Australia (+8.1%) are growing fastest. The Australian Capital Territory (+4.2%) and Victoria (+4.4%) are growing slowest.

Australia overall grew 5.7% in the 12 months to August 2026 compared with the 12 months before. These rates include price rises.

Chart 7 · How much is spent

Size tells a different story. New South Wales and Victoria make up 56% of tracked spending, about $44.5 billion of $79.8 billion a month. The Northern Territory makes up about 1%.

This map shows how much is spent in total, not how fast it is growing and not spending per person.

Chart 8 · Size and speed together

A high growth rate on a small base adds few dollars. New South Wales added the most, $14.8 billion (+5.1%), then Queensland ($12.2 billion) and Victoria ($9.8 billion).

The Northern Territory grew fastest but added $0.8 billion, just over 5% of New South Wales’s extra dollars. All eight areas together added $51.8 billion.

Chart 9 · How each state splits its spending

Every state spends on the same nine categories, in different proportions. In the Northern Territory, 23.2% of tracked spending goes on eating out (Australia: 13.8%) and only 6.7% on health (11.7%).

In Western Australia, 20.7% goes on transport (17.2%). Victoria puts more into recreation (18.4%) and health (12.9%) than the national average.

States differ in size, speed and shape. Part 4 steps back to ask what has shifted for households overall.

04

Shifting priorities

What matters more than it did 30 years ago, and what has not changed?

Chart 10 · Spending shares, 1995–96 and 2025–26

Housing took 20.0% of household spending in 1995–96 and 24.6% in 2025–26, a rise of 4.6 percentage points.

Health went from 4.8% to 7.4% and education from 1.9% to 3.6%. The biggest falls were transport (12.6% to 10.3%), furnishings (about 6% to 4%) and food (11.2% to 9.5%).

Chart 11 · The December effect

Spending is not the same every month, and December stands out. Compared with an average month, December spending on clothing is about 41% higher, alcohol and tobacco 28%, furnishings 21% and recreation 14%.

Health and transport are the exceptions, about 5% and 6% below an average month. All tracked spending is about 9% higher.

Chart 12 · Wants and needs

Tracked spending grew by about $425 billion from 2013 to 2026, and wants took about 66 cents of every extra dollar, matching their share of the total. Despite higher living costs, households have not been pushed toward essentials.

Housing, health and education gained share in chart 10, so food and transport likely fell by as much. What changed was the size of the pie, not how it is cut: wants grew 82% and needs 76%, too close to shift the split.

So, where does the money go?

Housing takes the biggest slice and has done in every year shown, and its share has grown. Health and education are climbing, while food, transport and furnishings take a smaller share than 30 years ago.

Spending can swing fast, as in April 2020 and every December. And one thing holds steady: about 65 cents of every tracked dollar go on wants, as they did in 2013.
