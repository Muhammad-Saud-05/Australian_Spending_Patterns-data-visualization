Consumer spending in Australia

Where does the money go?

Australian households now spend about $82 billion a month on the goods and services the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) tracks, about 35% more than in 2012 once price rises are removed. Twelve charts follow where it goes, how that has shifted over 30 years, how it differs from state to state, and what has stayed surprisingly steady.

Terms

Hover over any chart for exact figures. Where a chart can also be clicked, its subtitle says so.

Seasonally adjusted
Smoothed to remove the usual yearly pattern, such as the December rush, so one month can be compared fairly with another.
Current prices
Dollars of the day. Figures include price rises, so part of any increase is things costing more. Charts other than Chart 1 use current prices.
Price-adjusted
Past spending restated in August 2026 dollars, so a rise means people are buying more, not just paying more. Chart 1 uses this.
Tracked spending
What the ABS monthly indicator measures. It leaves out rent, education, communications, electricity and gas.
Wants and needs
“Wants” is our plain word for what the ABS calls discretionary spending, the kind people can more easily cut back on. “Needs” is the rest.
01

The big picture

How much do Australians spend, and on what?

Chart 1 · Total monthly spending

Spending has climbed from $61.1 billion a month in July 2012 to $82.3 billion in August 2026, up 35% after price rises are removed (all in August 2026 dollars). In the dollars of the day, the rise is 88%, from $43.7 billion, but much of that is prices.

The one sharp break came in 2020: spending fell from $67.6 billion in February to $52.6 billion in April, and was back above its February level by July.

Chart 2 · Where a dollar goes

Housing takes about a quarter of every dollar partly because of how it is counted: it includes an estimated rent that owners “pay” themselves, so about 21% of all spending is rent, real or estimated. Its share has also grown from 19% in 1985–86, likely as house prices and rents outpaced other costs.

Food has shrunk from 12.8% to 9.5%. People can only eat so much, so as incomes rise the extra goes to services such as eating out, now almost as big. Insurance, finance and other ranks second partly because it is a catch-all.

Housing leads. But has the order always looked like this? Part 2 follows the mix over time.

02

Composition over time

How has the mix of spending changed?

Chart 3 · Rank of each category, 40 years

The top of the ranking barely moves. Housing is first in every year shown, and insurance, finance and other has been second since 1990–91. The 2020–21 pandemic year scrambled the order, but most categories had returned to their usual places by 2025–26.

The real change is lower down. Education climbed from 12th to 9th, mostly in the latest period, and health from 9th to 7th, likely as the population ages and more people study. Food dropped from 3rd to 5th early on and has stayed there, while alcohol and tobacco hit its lowest rank, 11th.

Chart 4 · Monthly spending by category

Total tracked spending rose 70% since 2013, so eating out (+103%) and health (+102%) grew far faster than the rest. Eating out added the most dollars, about $5.4 billion a month, and now takes 13.8% of tracked spending, up from 11.5%. That likely reflects a return to dining out and higher menu prices.

Alcohol and tobacco is the odd one out. It is up only 11% since 2013 and down about a quarter from its 2022 peak ($47.8 billion to $35.5 billion a year), so its share has shrunk from 5.9% to 3.8%.

Chart 5 · The 2020 shock, cell by cell

The shock was uneven. In April 2020, eating out fell 56% and transport 54% from a year earlier, while food, furnishings and alcohol rose, likely as people stayed home. Eating out and transport took almost two years to regain their February 2020 level.

Alcohol and tobacco held up in 2020 and is now the exception: below a year earlier in 41 of the 44 months from January 2023 to August 2026. In August 2026 all nine categories were above a year earlier.

The mix moves, and a shock can move it fast. Part 3 asks whether every state moves together.

03

Across Australia

Does spending look the same in every state?

Chart 6 · How fast spending is growing

Five of the eight areas grew faster than Australia’s 5.7%, led by the Northern Territory (+8.2%) and Western Australia (+8.1%). The national figure is lower because New South Wales (+5.1%) and Victoria (+4.4%) hold more than half of all spending and grew more slowly.

The Northern Territory is growing from a very small base, and Western Australia’s pace likely reflects its resources sector. These rates include price rises, so faster growth does not always mean households are buying more.

Chart 7 · How much is spent

Spending is concentrated on the east coast. New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland account for 76% of the $79.8 billion spent each month. The Northern Territory, Tasmania and the ACT together make up about 5%, less than South Australia alone.

These totals most likely follow population, so the map shows where people live more than who spends most. It also lines up with chart 6: the biggest areas are among the slowest growing.

Chart 8 · Size and speed together

Rate and dollars rank areas differently. New South Wales added the most, $14.8 billion, but Western Australia grew from about half of Victoria’s base and added nearly as much, $8.4 billion against $9.8 billion.

Queensland and Western Australia hold a third of spending but added 40% of the extra dollars. New South Wales and Victoria hold 56% and added 47%. The Northern Territory grew fastest but added just 1.6% of the total.

Chart 9 · How each state splits its spending

States agree on the basics and differ on the rest. Food takes between 14% and 16% everywhere, but eating out ranges from 11% to 23%. In the Northern Territory, 23.2% goes on eating out (Australia: 13.8%) and only 6.7% on health (11.7%), which puts its wants share at 73%, the highest in the country.

That likely reflects hospitality, tourism and a younger, transient workforce. In Western Australia, 20.7% goes on transport (17.2%), probably because of long distances. Victoria spends the least on eating out but the most on recreation and health.

States differ in size, speed and shape. Part 4 steps back to ask what has shifted for households overall.

04

Shifting priorities

What matters more than it did 30 years ago, and what has not changed?

Chart 10 · Spending shares, 1995–96 and 2025–26

The big shift is housing. Its share was flat at about 20% until the mid-2000s, then climbed to 24.6%, so almost one household dollar in four now goes on it. Rising rents, mortgages and house prices are the most likely cause.

Health and education gained as well, likely reflecting an ageing population and more people studying for longer. Together with housing they gained 8.9 points, paid for by goods: transport, furnishings, food, recreation, alcohol and clothing lost 9.8. Transport’s slide began well before the pandemic.

Chart 11 · The December effect

December adds about $7 billion to an average month, and most of it comes from four categories. Clothing and recreation each add about $1.7 billion, with food and furnishings close to $1 billion each. This is most likely Christmas gifts, parties and summer sales.

Clothing has the biggest jump (+41%), but recreation adds nearly as many dollars because it is a far bigger category. Health and transport fall about 5% below an average month, probably as appointments and commuting pause over the holidays. The pattern repeats almost exactly in 2023, 2024 and 2025.

Chart 12 · Wants and needs

Tracked spending grew by about $425 billion from 2013 to 2026, and wants took about 66 cents of every extra dollar, matching their share of the total. Despite higher living costs, households have not been pushed toward essentials.

Housing, health and education gained share in chart 10, so food and transport likely fell by as much. What changed was the size of the pie, not how it is cut: wants grew 82% and needs 76%, too close to shift the split.

So, where does the money go?

Housing takes the biggest slice and has done in every year shown, and its share has grown. Health and education are climbing, while food, transport and furnishings take a smaller share than 30 years ago.

Spending can swing fast, as in April 2020 and every December. And one thing holds steady: about 65 cents of every tracked dollar go on wants, as they did in 2013.
